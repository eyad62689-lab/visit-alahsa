When the scent of lomi drifts through the homes of Al-Ahsa in summer, it feels as if this small green fruit has grown here since the beginning of time. Its true story is longer and travels further: it begins in the forests of Southeast Asia millions of years ago, passes through trading ships and Arab sailors, and ends in the groves of the oasis, where the [Hasawi lomi](/en/blog/hasawi-lomi-lime/) has become the second agricultural product after dates.

This article tells that journey with sources: every fact is paired with a link to its reference, and where something is unproven, we say so. The oasis needs no legend to be proud of its lime.

## What Is the Hasawi Lime, Botanically?

The Hasawi lime is not a species of its own, but a local landrace of the small acid lime known around the world by several names: Key lime, Mexican lime and West Indian lime, with the scientific name Citrus × aurantiifolia. In Oman it is the Omani lime, in Egypt the banzahir, and in eastern Arabia the lomi ([Wikipedia: Key lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_lime) and the [NC State Extension plant guide](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/citrus-x-aurantiifolia/)).

In official Saudi sources, the [Saudi Press Agency](https://www.spa.gov.sa/a2a7e0ca4bw) and [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/article/15994/) both record that the Hasawi lime "is called the Qatifi lime and Bin Zuhairi", meaning the three names belong to a single fruit.

The most important point about its identity: this "species" is not a true wild species but a **natural hybrid** of two wild parents, Citrus micrantha of the papeda group and Citrus medica, the citron, which is why its name carries the hybrid sign (×). Genomic analysis has confirmed this hybrid origin ([Wikipedia: Key lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_lime) and [Wu et al., Nature, 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature25447)).

This distinction is decisive for the history: the first hybridisation happened where both parents grew together, a tropical Asian environment, and neither parent grows wild in eastern Arabia. Every lomi tree in Al-Ahsa is therefore, by necessity, descended from a tree that arrived from outside.

## The First Homeland: Southeast Asia and the Himalayan Foothills

The landmark genomic study published in [Nature in 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature25447), based on 60 genomes representing the ancestral lineages of citrus, concluded that the genus diversified in the late Miocene through a rapid radiation in Southeast Asia, linked to a marked weakening of the monsoon.

A [later genomic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12203252/) narrows the centre of origin further: the southeastern Himalayan region spanning eastern Assam in India, western Yunnan in China and Myanmar, some 7 to 8 million years ago. The acid lime in particular is native to the tropics of Southeast Asia according to the [NC State Extension guide](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/citrus-x-aurantiifolia/).

The common phrase "it comes from India and the Malay Archipelago" is therefore imprecise: India was a corridor and a centre of dispersal, not the first homeland.

## The Journey West: 3 Waves Across a Thousand Years

Citrus did not reach the West in a single wave, but in several separated by centuries. The 2017 study [The Citrus Route Revealed](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318556307_The_Citrus_Route_Revealed_From_Southeast_Asia_into_the_Mediterranean) traced the sequence from botanical and archaeological remains:

- **The citron** moved west first, through Persia and the Levant; its remains were found in a Persian royal garden near Jerusalem dating to the 5th and 4th centuries BCE.
- **The lemon** followed; its oldest remains were found in the Roman Forum in the late 1st century BCE.
- **The acid lime**, together with the sour orange and the pomelo, did not reach the Mediterranean basin until the 10th century CE, as part of the great agricultural diffusion of the Islamic world.

This third wave is the heart of historian [Andrew Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Murray_Watson)'s thesis in Agricultural Innovation in the Early Islamic World (700 to 1100), which traced the spread of 17 food crops, the acid lime and the sour orange among them, through irrigation networks and agricultural knowledge in the first four centuries of Islamic rule.

## When Did It Reach Eastern Arabia?

Here begins the part where stories multiply and documents thin out, and honesty requires drawing its limits clearly.

**What is documented:** the best available account concerns the immediate neighbourhood, not Al-Ahsa itself. A [study published in SpringerPlus in 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5047875/) records that the Omani lime has been cultivated in Oman for at least 4 centuries, that it was brought there by Arab sailors, and that it remained Oman's second most important fruit crop after dates until the 1970s.

**What is not documented:** no document or historical text dates the arrival of the acid lime in the Al-Ahsa or Qatif oases specifically, and no published archaeobotanical evidence (seed remains or pollen) from archaeological sites in eastern Arabia proves the presence of citrus in any particular period. The claim that citrus entered both oases at the same time is a reasonable inference from their shared environment and trade network, not an established fact.

The likeliest reading, then, is that the lomi reached the Gulf coast the same way it reached Oman: by sea, with merchants and sailors, at a date we cannot yet fix.

## Al-Ahsa or Qatif? A Question That Needs No Quarrel

It is sometimes said that the tree originated in one of the two oases and then spread to the other. That claim fails on 3 independent grounds:

1. **Botanical:** the species is a hybrid of Asian origin and neither wild parent exists in eastern Arabia, so a local origin is biologically impossible, not merely unlikely ([Wikipedia: Key lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_lime)).
2. **Linguistic:** the two names belong to one fruit, as the [Saudi Press Agency](https://www.spa.gov.sa/a2a7e0ca4bw) and [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/article/15994/) confirm; the difference is in the name, not the plant.
3. **Genetic:** the acid lime across the region is propagated from very closely related stock. An [AFLP analysis of acid lime in Oman](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29527419/) found its genetic diversity so low that this was suggested as a factor in the spread of witches' broom disease. Low diversity means, in practice, that the region's trees are near-copies of a single genetic stock, not distinct landraces that each arose in its own oasis.

So what remains for the two oases? Something worth more than a claim of origin: centuries of local agricultural selection, an irrigation system and palm shade that created a microclimate of their own, inherited know-how in pruning, sun-drying and ageing, and a complete food culture crowned by the custom of jameed. That is what distinction is built on, not the tree of origin.

As for the differences attributed to the two oases' produce in peel thickness, acidity and essential oils, they are an environmentally plausible hypothesis, but no published comparative study has measured them, so they remain a matter of likelihood.

## Where Does the Name Bin Zuhairi Come From?

The fruit's very name carries a trace of its journey. The word banzahir is of Persian origin and means **antidote**, a meaning recorded explicitly in [Al-Mu'jam Al-Wasit](https://www.almaany.com/ar/dict/ar-ar/بنزهير/). Its compound root is badzahr: bad meaning "against" and zahr meaning "poison", the same word that entered European languages as bezoar, the antidote stone ([dictionary of foreign loanwords in Arabic](https://mbtda.com/language/a3jmi/baa.php)).

The significance runs deeper than it looks: the fruit's name is Persian in freight, one more sign that it arrived through a trading and linguistic network spanning the region, rather than sprouting locally and being named locally. Lomi, meanwhile, is a general Gulf name, and Hasawi and Qatifi are geographical labels for the market, not for the landrace.

## The Lomi Today: From Imported Fruit to Local Identity

The fruit that came from afar is today the emblem of the oasis summer. The Hasawi lomi is officially classed as Al-Ahsa's second agricultural product after dates ([Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/article/15994/)), and the [Saudi Press Agency](https://www.spa.gov.sa/a2a7e0ca4bw) records more than 100,000 bearing trees in the oasis, each yielding 25 to 30 kilograms a season. Tree counts do vary between official sources and some [press coverage](https://www.alwatan.com.sa/article/1084098); we use the official agency's figure here, as on our [Fruits of Al-Ahsa](/en/fruits/) page.

Culturally, Hasawi families gather every summer to press and store the lime, most visibly as jameed: lomi juice sun-dried and aged in glass jars for use all year round, a custom handed down through the generations ([Akhbaar 24](https://www.akhbaar24.com/article/detail/615953)). The celebration has grown to the point that the fruit has its own annual [Hasawi Lomi Exhibition](/en/events/hasawi-lomi-exhibition/), and picking it from the tree has become a visitor experience at the [Hasawi Lemon Farm](/en/attractions/lemon-farm/).

## A Threat to the Heritage: Witches' Broom Disease

The lomi's story is incomplete without its greatest danger. Witches' broom disease of lime is caused by a phytoplasma known as Candidatus Phytoplasma aurantifolia, first detected in Oman in the 1970s, which then spread to the United Arab Emirates in 1989 and Iran in the 1990s, destroying more than a million lime trees ([European Food Safety Authority report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7009860/)). Oman's lime production fell by more than 50% over 3 decades, chiefly because of it ([Plant Pathology, 2017](https://bsppjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ppa.12683)).

What matters most for Al-Ahsa: a [study published in Plant Disease in 2021](https://apsjournals.apsnet.org/doi/10.1094/PDIS-10-20-2112-RE) showed that phytoplasma isolates taken from the Al-Ahsa region are genetically distinct from their counterparts elsewhere in the Middle East. Earlier, a Saudi study from 2009 (Alhudaib et al.) documented the phytoplasma associated with lime decline in the Kingdom and its likely vectors, as the [same European report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7009860/) notes.

This explains why the lomi's agricultural heritage in Al-Ahsa is at once precious and fragile: a landrace of low genetic diversity in a region facing a disease that has destroyed a million trees next door.

## The Bottom Line

- The Hasawi lime is Citrus × aurantiifolia, a natural hybrid of Citrus micrantha and Citrus medica, neither a true species nor a variety of local origin.
- Its first homeland is Southeast Asia and the eastern Himalayan foothills, not India alone and not the Arabian Peninsula.
- It reached the Mediterranean in the 10th century CE with the agricultural diffusion of the Islamic world; its arrival in the Gulf most likely came by sea and trade, as witnessed by at least 4 centuries of cultivation in Oman.
- Hasawi lime, Qatifi lime, Bin Zuhairi and lomi are names for one fruit, as official Saudi sources confirm.
- There is no basis for attributing the origin to either Al-Ahsa or Qatif; the two oases' real value lies in their accumulated agricultural and cultural heritage.
