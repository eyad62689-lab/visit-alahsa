# Follow-up answers — جبل القارہ (reader-06)

Same reader, same page, nothing else consulted. For each point I say first whether it caught my eye on the first read.

## 1. The plural of "cave" and its agreement

**First read: no, it did not catch my eye.** It read naturally and I passed over it without stopping.

The page treats the word as masculine throughout, with the plural identical to the singular in the direct case: «وقت کے تراشے ہوئے غار، جو گرمیوں میں ٹھنڈے رہتے ہیں», «غار اور راستے بل کھاتے چلے گئے ہیں», «جبل القارہ کے غار … مشہور ہیں», and then «ان غاروں کا درجہ حرارت» in the oblique. All of that is consistent with itself, and it is what I say and write myself: «غار ٹھنڈے ہیں», «بڑے بڑے غار», «غاروں میں». The word is fixed as masculine in my ear by «غارِ حرا» and «غارِ ثور».

I do hear «غاریں» (feminine plural, «غاریں ٹھنڈی ہیں») from some speakers, and I would understand it without effort, but it is not what comes out of my own mouth and on a written page it would look colloquial to me. So nothing here needs changing for a reader like me.

One small thing I notice only now that you ask: because the plural looks the same as the singular, «غار» in «چٹانوں کے بیچ غار اور راستے …» is only shown to be plural by the verb at the end. It did not trip me, since «راستے» beside it is visibly plural.

## 2. Opening hours: «8:00–22:00» and «8:00 سے 22:00 تک»

**First read: yes, this caught my eye** (it is item 12 in my list).

It is not what I expect on an Urdu page. I can read «22:00» — living in the Gulf I see the 24-hour clock on tickets and signs — but I read it as a foreign format and convert it in my head. In Urdu running text I expect the 12-hour clock with the part of the day named: «صبح 8 بجے سے رات 10 بجے تک». In a compact card cell I would also find «صبح 8 بجے تا رات 10 بجے» perfectly normal; «تا» is the short range word I am used to seeing on shop boards and timetables.

Of the two forms on the page, the FAQ one («8:00 سے 22:00 تک») is the easier, because «سے … تک» tells me which end is the start. The card form («8:00–22:00») with a bare dash made me hesitate for a moment over the direction of the range in a right-to-left line. In both, the foreign part is the «22:00» itself, not the «:00».

## 3. Price: «50 سعودی ریال (SAR)» and «50 SAR»

**First read: yes, both caught my eye** (items 14 and 19).

«50 سعودی ریال (SAR)» — the first three words read exactly right and I had the price. Then the bracket made me pause: Latin capitals in the middle of an Urdu line, telling me again what I had just read in full. I took it as something left over from an English version, not as help for me.

«50 SAR» — this read as a code, not a price. I know «SAR» from exchange-house slips and bank apps, so I understood it, but nobody says it aloud; I say «پچاس ریال». A relative in Pakistan reading the same page might not know the code at all, and in the FAQ there is no Urdu word beside it. It also jars that the same price is written two different ways thirty lines apart.

What I expect to see: «50 ریال» (on a page that is plainly about Saudi Arabia that is enough), or «50 سعودی ریال» if the page wants to be explicit. The same form in both places.

## 4. The spelling «الاحساء»

**First read: no, it did not catch my eye.** I recognised it at once and read it as "al-Ahsaa" without thinking about the spelling.

Asked now: yes, it is a spelling I recognise and it looks like Urdu to me. The other written form I would find equally normal is «الاحسا» without the final hamza — Urdu often drops that hamza after alif in words taken from Arabic (the way «علماء» and «علما» both appear), so I would not be surprised by either and could not tell you which is "the" spelling. What would look un-Urdu to me is the Arabic form with a hamza on the first alif («الأحساء»); that I would read as an Arabic word quoted in an Urdu page.

In speech, among Pakistanis here, I mostly hear the shorter "al-Hasa", but I would not expect to see that on a website; I would take it as the spoken name.

The page is consistent: it is «الاحساء» every time it appears (three times in the paragraph, once in the card). That consistency matters more to me than which of the two written forms is chosen.

## 5. The parenthesis in «جبل القارہ (القارہ پہاڑ)»

**First read: yes, this caught my eye** (item 1).

I took it to be telling me one thing only: that «جبل» means «پہاڑ» — a translation of the name for a reader who does not know the Arabic word. I did not read it as a second name the place goes by, and I did not read it as help with pronunciation, because «القارہ» is repeated inside the bracket in exactly the same unvowelled form.

For me it explained the half of the name I already knew and left alone the half I did not: I still do not know what «القارہ» means, whether it is the name of a village or a word with a meaning, or how exactly to say it. For a split second I did wonder whether «القارہ پہاڑ» was meant as "the name people use in Urdu", because it is set out like an alternative name; then I decided it was only a gloss, since it keeps the Arabic «ال» and is not put together the way Urdu names a mountain.

It also turned out not to be needed: the same sentence goes on «سر اٹھائے کھڑا ہے», and two sentences later the text says «پہاڑ کی تنگ راہداریوں», so the page itself makes clear that this is a mountain without the bracket.
